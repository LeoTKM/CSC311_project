# Plotting report: how the figures are built

This report documents the plotting script in `data_visualization/` — what it
encodes, what it draws, and how to read the result. It is written by hand; nothing
here is generated by a script.

- `plot_histograms.py` → `figures/00_overview.png` … `figures/09_count_Q7.png`
- `plot_payment.py` → `figures/10_payment_Q12.png`, `figures/11_payment_Q12_trimmed.png`

---

## 1. Data source

Every figure is drawn from the **train split only** (`data/splits/train.csv`,
606 rows over 202 `id` groups, 202 rows per class). The split is grouped by `id`
(all rows of one respondent stay together), so val and test cannot leak into the
picture. The full dataset (`data/class_dataset.csv`, 870 rows) is never plotted.

The one exception in input, not in policy, is the Q12 figure: since the payment
question is encoded into two numeric columns, `10_payment_Q12.png` reads the encoded
train split `data/encoded/train_encoded.csv` (also 606 rows) instead of the raw
`data/splits/train.csv`. Both scripts refuse to read a validation or test file.

Each respondent answered the survey three times — once per sport — so a figure
showing "606 respondents" really shows 606 responses from 202 people. This matters
when reading absolute counts: they are response counts, not independent people.

---

## 2. Feature encoding methods

The survey mixes five question types, and each type needs its own encoding before it
can be plotted or fed to a model.

### 2.1 Q1 — ease of starting (0–10 numeric scale)

The raw column mixes integers, decimals (`7.5`, `3.5`) and blanks. Encoding: parse to
a float, and treat blanks as missing. The histogram figures do not use Q1.

### 2.2 Q2–Q4 — 5-point Likert (single choice)

Mapped to an **ordinal score 1–5**, which is the encoding used everywhere:

| Label | Score |
|---|--:|
| Strongly disagree | 1 |
| Disagree | 2 |
| Neutral | 3 |
| Agree | 4 |
| Strongly agree | 5 |

Why ordinal rather than one-hot: the five levels have a natural order, and the mean
of the score reads directly as an "attitude score" while the variance reads as the
spread of opinion. The assumption is that the levels are roughly equally spaced.
The histogram figures show the individual levels as well, so a non-monotone pattern
(e.g. a class peaking at "Neutral") is still visible even though the encoding is
one-dimensional. Blanks are excluded from the distributions.

### 2.3 Q5–Q7 — multi-select (checkbox)

Each option becomes its own **0/1 indicator (multi-hot)**: 7 options for venue,
4 for season, 10 for music type, i.e. 21 binary features. A response that ticks
several options therefore produces several ones.

Why not a single ordinal number: the options have no order (`Indoor = 1, Outdoor = 2`
would invent a ranking) and a response can hold several options at once, so a single
number would destroy information.

An additional **count feature** ("number of options selected", one per question) is
derived as a real number valued 0…7 / 0…4 / 0…10. It carries the *quantity* of
selection while the multi-hot columns carry *which* options were selected. For these
questions only counts are reported because for a 0/1
indicator the variance is fully determined by the mean, so the moments add nothing
over the raw counts.

### 2.4 Q8–Q11 — short answers (free text)

Q8 to Q11 are counted as words, not yet turned into model features. Q12 is the fifth
short answer but it is a price question, so it is parsed into an amount instead — see
§2.5. Normalisation for Q8–Q11:

1. lowercase;
2. drop apostrophes (`don't` → `dont`, including the typographic `’`);
3. repair thousands separators (`1,000` → `1000`);
4. replace every other non-alphanumeric character (punctuation, `$`, `/`, `-`,
   commas) with a space, then split on spaces.

So `Hot dog, soda` → `hot dog soda`, `$1,000` → `1000`, `Hip-hop or rap` →
`hip hop or rap`, `N/A` → `na` (an
`N/A` answer counts as one word, not as the two tokens `n` and `a`). Because step 4 only keeps `[a-z0-9]`, any
non-Latin character also acts as a word boundary. Single-character tokens are kept
by default (`--min-len 1`).

### 2.5 Q12 — willingness to pay (short answer parsed to an amount)

Q12 is the only short answer that is not counted as words: it asks for a price, so
`data_preprocessing/encode_all.py` parses it into two numeric columns instead of a
TF-IDF block.

- `payment_amount` — the parsed amount as a float; replies with no usable amount are
  filled with the **training-split mean** (274.20), which is fitted on train only and
  then reused unchanged for val and test.
- `payment_missing` — 1 when the reply held no parseable amount, 0 otherwise.

The parser keeps one number, or exactly two numbers joined by `-` / `to` (the range
midpoint is taken, so `400-500` becomes 450), and repairs thousands separators
(`1,000` → `1000`). It rejects: word-only amounts (`Hundreds of dollars`,
`Couple hundred bucks`), open scales (`1 to 10 scale ... I will put a 2`), more than
two numbers (several scenarios in one answer), and negative amounts. A reply that
states unwillingness or `None` carries no number, so it also counts as missing.

**Currency.** Amounts are read as CAD. Currency notation (the dollar sign, `CAD`,
`USD`, `EUR`, `dollars`, `bucks`) is stripped **without any exchange-rate
conversion**, so every notation contributes its bare number as a CAD amount. In the
train split 57 of 606 replies (9.4%) have no parseable amount.

Because the amounts are strongly right-skewed (median 100, mean 274, max 10000),
equal-width bins would put almost every response in the first bar. The figure
therefore uses 8 round-amount buckets — `0`, `1-25`, `25-50`, `50-100`, `100-200`,
`200-500`, `500-1000`, `1000+` — plus a separate `not stated` bucket for the imputed
replies. The buckets are drawn as categorical positions, not as a numeric axis,
because the bins are not equally wide and a numeric axis would draw the wrong widths.

### 2.6 Missing and invalid values

Two different counts are tracked per feature:

- **blank** — the cell is empty (question not answered);
- **invalid** — the answer cannot be used under that question type's rules
  (not a number in 0–10; not one of the 5 Likert labels; an option outside the
  closed vocabulary; a "no information" reply such as `none` / `n/a` / `nothing`
  for the short answers; for the price question, neither an amount nor a stated
  unwillingness).

The histograms drop blanks (they are not a category), and the per-class `n` in each
legend or footnote is the number of usable responses.

## 3. Plotting methods

### 3.1 Grouped-bar histograms for the categorical questions

Twelve figures (01–11 plus the overview) use the same grouped-bar construction:

- the **x axis** is the discrete bin of the question: the 1–5 Likert levels, the
  individual options of a multi-select question, or the possible values of the
  "number of options selected" count;
- for every bin, **three bars side by side**, one per class (Soccer green,
  Baseball blue, Basketball orange), with a thin white edge so adjacent bars stay
  distinguishable;
- the **y axis** is the number of responses ("Number of respondents (count)"), so
  the three classes can be compared directly — they are the same size (202 each);
- **every bar is labelled** with its count; for the multi-select figures the label has
  a second line with the within-class percentage (`276` / `96%`), i.e. "how many of
  that class's respondents ticked this option";
- a **legend** names the three classes (no numbers in it); a one-line **footnote**
  states what the bar height and label mean, how many valid answers the question has,
  and where the per-class tables live.

Grouped bars were chosen over overlapping translucent histograms because the three
distributions of one bin then stand side by side at reading scale, and because each
bar can carry its own numeric label without the labels colliding.

The single-choice figures also carry a compact `mean: … variance: …` line in the
title (order Soccer / Baseball / Basketball), which lets the reader see the summary
statistics without leaving the figure. The multi-select and count figures do not:
they show raw counts only.

### 3.2 Overview figure

`00_overview.png` is a 2 × 3 grid: the top row repeats the three single-choice
histograms (5 levels each), the bottom row shows the three multi-select option
distributions (7 / 4 / 10 options). It is a one-glance summary of all six categorical
questions; because the panels are small, the single-choice panels carry per-class
mean and variance as two extra title lines instead of a per-bar block, the option
labels of the bottom row are rotated, and one shared legend is placed under the
figure.

### 3.3 Q12 payment histogram

`10_payment_Q12.png` follows the same grouped-bar construction but with three
deliberate differences:

- the **x axis** is the 9 amount buckets of §2.5, the last one (`not stated`) shaded
  grey and separated by a dashed line so it is never read as an amount;
- the **y axis** is still the number of responses, and the label under each bar is the
  count plus the **within-class percentage of the whole class** (denominator 202), so
  the 9 buckets add up to 100% even though some replies have no amount;
- the **title** carries the summary statistics instead of a compact mean/variance pair,
  because seven statistics are needed per class: the `x / y / z` order is always
  Soccer / Baseball / Basketball. It reports, in this order: `missing` count,
  `fill value` (the train mean, a single number because it is shared by all three
  classes), `IQR (Q1-Q3)`, `max`, `min`, `mean` and `variance`. Variance is printed in
  scientific notation (`1.19e6`) because raw-dollar variance reaches seven digits and
  is driven by a handful of extreme answers — its square root is the standard
  deviation (1088.58 / 327.53 / 573.38).

Median and IQR are deliberately reported alongside mean and variance: with this much
skew, `mean: 411.58` for Soccer describes almost nobody, while `IQR 92-400` describes
the middle half of that class.

### 3.4 Q12 histogram after dropping the upper tail

`11_payment_Q12_trimmed.png` is the same data with the extreme amounts removed, so that
mean and variance become usable. The rule is applied **inside each class**: an amount is
dropped when it exceeds that class's own `Q3 + 1.5 x IQR` (the usual upper fence), so no
class is measured against another class's scale.

Only the **upper** tail is cut. Every amount from 0 up to the cut is kept, which means
the `$0` answers — respondents who would not pay at all — are never removed, even
though they sit far below `Q1`. Trimming both tails would have deleted the strongest
signal in the feature.

| | Soccer | Baseball | Basketball |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| Cut = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR | 861 | 205 | 475 |
| Dropped | 22 | 14 | 26 |
| Kept (of parsed) | 160 / 182 | 168 / 182 | 159 / 185 |
| Kept range | 0 – 625 | 0 – 200 | 0 – 400 |
| Mean after the drop | 188.80 | 68.47 | 146.39 |
| Variance after the drop | 2.47e4 | 2.86e3 | 1.07e4 |
| Mean before the drop | 411.58 | 125.84 | 285.01 |
| Variance before the drop | 1.19e6 | 1.07e5 | 3.29e5 |

Two things are worth reading off this. First, the class order survives the trim
(Soccer > Basketball > Baseball), so the difference between classes is not an artefact
of a few huge answers. Second, the variance collapses by a factor of 48 / 38 / 31, which
is why the untrimmed mean was untrustworthy: the two 10000 answers in Soccer alone move
that class's mean by about 106 (a single one moves it by 53).

**Why this figure can use a continuous axis and figure 10 cannot.** Trimming changes the
shape, not just the range:

| | Soccer | Baseball | Basketball |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| Skew, all parsed amounts | 7.69 | 7.66 | 6.44 |
| Skew, after the drop | 0.96 | 0.92 | 0.82 |
| mean / median, after the drop | 1.26 | 1.37 | 1.22 |

At a skew near 0.9 an equal-width binning no longer puts almost everything in the first
bar, so the x axis can be a real numeric scale with **equal 50 CAD bins** — bar width
then equals bin width, which is what makes a numeric axis honest. Figure 10's round
amount buckets (`1-25`, `200-500`, …) differ in width by a factor of 12 and must stay
categorical.

**The three panels do not share an x axis.** The trimmed ranges differ by a factor of
three (Baseball stops at 200, Soccer reaches 625), so one shared axis would squeeze
Baseball into its left third: with 13 bins on a common 0–650 axis, Baseball fills only
5, while 86% of Baseball is at or below 100. Each panel therefore gets its own horizontal
axis — 13 bins for Soccer, 4 for Baseball, 8 for Basketball — and only the **height**
axis is shared, so bar heights stay directly comparable:

```
bin (CAD)        0-50  50-100  100-150  150-200  200-250  250-300  300-350  350-400  400-450  450-500  500-550  550-600  600-650
Soccer (n=160)     20      26       33        7       27        5       15        2        7        0       14        1        3
Baseball (n=168)   55      56       37       20        -        -        -        -        -        -        -        -        -
Basketball (n=159) 22      21       39       23       27        7        9       11        -        -        -        -        -
```

No bin holds more than a third of its class (21% / 33% / 25%), so the shape is readable
at this width; a width of 25 was rejected because half its bins fall empty on the round
amounts (50, 100, 200, 500) that respondents prefer, and a width of 100 was rejected
because it puts 66% of Baseball in one bar. The last bin of each panel is closed on both
ends, so a class maximum sitting exactly on the top edge (Baseball 200, Basketball 400)
is not silently dropped.

Two details follow from leaving the categorical axis. Replies with **no parseable
amount** have no position on a numeric scale, so they are reported as a text box inside
each panel (`not stated: 20`) instead of as a bar, and they are not trimmed. And an
amount that was removed simply does not appear anywhere in its panel, so a short right
tail means "everything above the cut was dropped", not "nobody answered that".

### 3.5 Style conventions

- **English only** in every figure.
- **Class order everywhere**: Soccer / Baseball / Basketball, with the same colours
  as the tables and the terminal output (Soccer `#2ca02c`, Baseball `#1f77b4`,
  Basketball `#ff7f0e`).
- **Titles carry the question**: `Q<number> | <full question wording>`, followed by
  the question type and, where relevant, the class means/variances.
- **Layout is guaranteed not to clip**: figures are saved with
  `bbox_inches="tight"` and a small padding, and the layout was checked so that no
  title, legend or footnote touches the canvas edge.
- Resolution: 200 dpi for the individual histograms, 170 dpi for the overview.

## 4. Reading the figures

| Figure | Question to ask while looking |
|---|---|
| `01`–`03` (single choice) | Do the three distributions shift monotonically, or does one class sit in the middle? Are the means close while the shapes differ (a sign that one-hot may beat the ordinal encoding)? |
| `04`–`06` (multi-select) | Which options are near-deterministic for one class (e.g. `Indoor facility`: Basketball 97 % vs Baseball 26 %)? Which options are flat across classes and therefore uninformative? |
| `07`–`09` (counts) | Does one class tick many more options than the others? The whole distribution is shown, per class. |
| `10` (Q12 payment) | Do the classes sit at different price points? Baseball is concentrated in `1-50` (46% of that class), Basketball peaks at `100-200`, Soccer has the longest right tail (`500-1000` holds 9.4%). How large is the `not stated` bar (about 9% of every class)? |
| `11` (Q12 trimmed) | One panel per class on its own continuous axis. Does the class order survive the trim (it does), do the means become believable (188.80 / 68.47 / 146.39 instead of 411.58 / 125.84 / 285.01), and where does each class actually sit — Baseball 0–200, Basketball 0–400, Soccer 0–625? |
| `00` | Overall shape of the problem in one glance. |

## 5. Known limitations

1. **Ordinal assumption** for Likert scores: treating the labels as equally spaced is
   an approximation. The level-by-level histograms expose where it breaks, and the
   analysis report also prints the full level counts.
2. **Text is not encoded** in any figure yet, so the four text questions Q8–Q11 do not
   appear in the plots even though they carry strong signals (e.g. the word `green`
   appears in 99 Soccer responses and never in a Basketball one). Q12 is the exception:
   it is plotted as a parsed amount, not as text.
3. **The Q12 figure hides the imputation.** The `not stated` bar accounts for the
   filled rows, but the figure cannot show what their real answer was. Part of that
   group is not missing information at all: in the train split 20 of the 57 filled
   replies say they are unwilling, unwillingness or `None`, which arguably means 0
   rather than "no amount" (8 of them are Baseball, 7 Basketball, 5 Soccer). Counting
   those as 0 instead of the 274.20 fill value would move the class means to 391.19 /
   129.67 / 274.59 — a shift of about 8% for Baseball and 2-3% for the other two. The
   imputation rule is therefore a modelling choice to revisit, not a neutral default.
4. **The trim in figure 11 is a descriptive device, not a preprocessing step.** The cut
   is fitted on the train split, so applying it to val / test would have to be done
   with the train cut and recorded in the encoder — throwing away 22 / 14 / 26 answers
   before training is a modelling decision that has to be justified, not a side effect
   of drawing a nicer picture.
5. **Histograms are marginal views, not evidence about models.** Each figure shows one
   feature against the three classes; the interactions between features are invisible
   here. Model choice still has to come from cross-validation grouped by `id`.

## 6. Reproducing the figures

```bash
python data_visualization/plot_histograms.py          # figures 00-09 from the train split
python data_visualization/plot_payment.py             # figures 10-11 from the encoded train split
```

`plot_histograms.py` accepts `--data` (default `data/splits/train.csv`) and `--out-dir`
(default `data_visualization/figures/`). `plot_payment.py` accepts `--data`
(default `data/encoded/train_encoded.csv`) and `--out-dir`, and always writes both Q12
figures. Figure 10-11 need the encoded split, so run
`python data_preprocessing/encode_all.py --input data/class_dataset.csv`
first if `data/encoded/train_encoded.csv` is missing.
